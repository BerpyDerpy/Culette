    23:00Light sleep, nearly

    Three nights a week, for three months. That has a name.

    Chronic insomnia is a medical condition. It isn't a personality trait, and it isn't a phase you have to wait out. Around one in ten adults lives with it.

    It is also very treatable. Most people never get treated, because nobody tells them a treatment exists.

    00:00Light sleep

    Things you've probably tried at this hour.

    1. Counting backwards from a thousand.
    2. Lavender on the pillow.
    3. Melatonin from the supermarket.
    4. No screens after nine.
    5. A podcast about the fall of Rome.
    6. One more glass of wine.
    7. Lying very still and trying harder.

    None of these are bad ideas, and a few help a little. None of them treats chronic insomnia, though, and the last two make it worse.

    CBT-I

    Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia

    The American College of Physicians and the American Academy of Sleep Medicine both recommend it as the first treatment for chronic insomnia, before sleeping pills. Its effects tend to last after treatment ends, which pills can't promise.

    01:00Deep sleep

    Six weeks. One clinician. Quieter nights.

    Once a week you talk to your clinician for 25 minutes, by video or phone. Every morning you spend two minutes on a sleep diary. You don't need a wearable or a subscription, and there are no streaks.

    1. Week 01Notice

      Write the nights down

      A two-minute diary each morning. How you remember the night matters more than anything a tracker records.

    2. Week 02Narrow

      Shrink the window

      It sounds backwards, and it's the part that works. You spend less time in bed for a while, so the sleep you do get runs deeper.

    3. Week 03Retrain

      Give the bed back to sleep

      If you've been awake for what feels like twenty minutes, get up and sit somewhere dim until you're sleepy. Your brain relearns what the bed is for.

    4. Week 04Unhook

      Answer the 3am voice

      The thoughts that turn up at night follow patterns. You'll learn to spot them, and to argue back.

    5. Week 05Widen

      Open the window again

      As your sleep consolidates, your time in bed grows in fifteen-minute steps, at the pace your diary says you're ready for.

    6. Week 06Keep

      Plan for bad nights

      Bad nights will still happen. You'll leave with a written plan for them, so one bad night doesn't turn into a bad month.

      06:00Dreaming, lightly

      Questions people ask before they start.

      Do I have to stop my sleeping pills?

      No, and please don't stop anything suddenly. Keep taking what you've been prescribed. Many people decide to taper later with their prescriber's help, often once CBT-I is working. Your clinician can talk to your doctor if that would help.

      Is this right for everyone?

      Not without changes. Parts of the program need adapting if you have bipolar disorder, epilepsy, untreated sleep apnea, or a job where drowsiness is dangerous, such as driving or operating machinery. Tell us in the assessment. Your clinician will tailor the program, or point you to care that suits you better.

      What if it's sleep apnea, not insomnia?

      Loud snoring, gasping at night, morning headaches and dozing off during the day can all point to sleep apnea. The assessment screens for it. If it looks likely, we'll refer you for a sleep study before we start anything else.

      I'm in my seventies. Will it still work?

      Yes. CBT-I works well for older adults, and it avoids the side effects that make sleeping pills riskier with age. Every session can be done by ordinary phone call, and we'll post you a large-print paper diary if you'd like one.

      How much time does it take each week?

      About 25 minutes with your clinician, plus two minutes each morning for the diary. The hard part isn't the time. Weeks two and three can leave you sleepier than usual while your sleep resets, and your clinician will help you plan around that.

      Who sees my information?

      Your clinician and the small clinical team who support them. We don't sell data and we don't run ads. Your employer never sees anything about you, even if they pay for the program.
